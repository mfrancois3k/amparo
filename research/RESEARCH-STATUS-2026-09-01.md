## THE FIVE BIGGEST FINDINGS OF THIS SESSION

### 1. Rhode Island §31-21.2-5 — six product questions in one section

The **Comprehensive Community-Police Relationship Act of 2015** answers, in a
single section: detention cap (task-anchored, tighter than RI's own two-hour
§12-7-1); K-9 timing; consent to search; document-demand cap; pretext
documentation; and a **statutory exclusionary remedy**. Plus mandatory dashcam
from the moment the officer signals.

**Thirty-five prior passes missed it** because every column was searched by its
own subject, and this lives in a *police-relations chapter of the motor-vehicle
title*. Method changed as a result — see below.

### 2. New Hampshire RSA 595-A:10 — the most product-relevant text found

Before a consent search is lawful the officer must expressly tell the driver:
they may refuse; refusal is **not** probable cause or reasonable suspicion;
they **cannot be charged** for refusing; they **cannot be further detained** for
refusing. On refusal the officer **shall cease questioning**. Consent must be on
a signed form or video-and-sound recording. Breach ⇒ **evidence inadmissible**.

Everything else in thirteen columns tells a driver what an officer may do. New
Hampshire answers *"if I say no, does it get worse?"* — three times over.

### 3. Virginia forecloses the consent cure — the sharpest drafting found

Eight sections carry the same formula: **no stop for this violation**, and
evidence is inadmissible **"including evidence discovered or obtained with the
operator's consent."**

Covers window tint, defective equipment, tail/plate lights, objects hung from the
mirror, seat belt, expired registration, expired inspection, and (broader still)
**the odour of marijuana** — which reaches stop, search, seizure *and* warrant.

Registration and inspection carry a **computable grace period**: no stop before
the first day of the **fourth month** after expiry. The only protections in the
project a driver can resolve to a calendar date.

### 4. The remedy tiers — the duty does not tell you the protection

Colorado and New Hampshire impose nearly the same duty. The protection is not
remotely the same.

| Tier | Remedy | States |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | exclusion **surviving consent** | VA (8 sections) |
| **1** | exclusion | NH, RI, CT |
| **2** | "a factor" in voluntariness | CO |
| **3** | none named | the data/policy statutes |

Colorado's statute is the **earliest** of them (2010) and its own Supreme Court
held that breach *"is not determinative."*

**Rule adopted: no cell records a duty without recording the remedy.**

### 5. Myth-kills and contamination

**Duration myths dead:** Colorado's widely-cited 90-minute cap **does not exist**
in §16-3-103. Virginia's 30-minute cap was **repealed in 1994**. Any claim of the
form *"they can only hold you N minutes"* is presumptively wrong until read.

**Eight instances of bill-text contamination** — text from unenacted bills
circulating as code (TN, NJ, TX, AZ, AL, MS, GA, NY). The New York one is the
most seductive: S9840/S3662A describe exactly an NH-style advisement, in a large
state, and surface on any keyword search.

**Florida §943.1714 is a phantom** — the index runs 943.1701 → 943.171 →
943.1715. Not repealed, not a bill: a number that never existed.

## METHOD FINDINGS THAT CHANGED HOW THIS IS RESEARCHED

1. **Check every state for a police-practices / community-relations / profiling
   chapter and read it in full**, whatever column is being worked. The columns
   are the product's questions, not the statute book's organisation. This is what
   found Rhode Island.

2. **A protection's position in the structure of the law matters more than its
   existence.** Twice-proven: WV's definitional carve-out beat six states'
   affirmative defences; NH's exclusion beat CO's identical duty.

3. **Consent and pretext are one question from opposite ends.** Consent asks *may
   they ask, and how?* Pretext asks *may they be here at all?* Virginia answers
   the second and moots the first.

4. **Sixteen trap classes documented.** Two added this session: DUI implied-consent
   statutes (all 50 states have one, they all say "consent", all irrelevant here)
   and search engines inventing content for real section numbers.

5. **The browser-pane hazard is the most dangerous failure mode in the tooling** —
   it yields *real statutory text from the wrong jurisdiction*, which passes every
   content check. Fired three times. No wrong cell is known to have entered, but
   every catch was a scout noticing, not the process preventing.

### The scope decision that needs a human

**Three of the largest states protect drivers by case law, not statute** — New
York (*People v. De Bour*), New Jersey (*State v. Carty*), Minnesota (*State v.
Fort*). This matrix is built from statutes. A product shipping only the statutory
answer will tell a NY, NJ or MN driver they have **no protection when they do**.

That is a product-scope call, not a research call.
